HRV for Runners: What Garmin, COROS, and Apple Watch Measure — and When to Rest

Updated August 2026 · 14 min read

Your Garmin HRV Status is unbalanced. COROS recovery is at 38%. Athlytic on Apple Watch flashed yellow. You have 6×1K at 5K pace on the plan. Skip or go?

Heart rate variability (HRV) is one of the few wearable metrics with a real physiology story — beat-to-beat interval variation tracks autonomic balance, and that balance shifts with training load, sleep, illness, and life stress. The problem is not that HRV is fake. The problem is treating a single morning number like a veto when the useful signal lives in trends, baselines, and context with resting heart rate and sleep.

This guide covers what RMSSD and SDNN actually mean, how Garmin/COROS/Apple measure overnight HRV differently, a practical ±1 standard deviation rule for hard sessions, and when to rest versus when to ignore the dashboard.

What HRV Measures (Without the Marketing)

Each heartbeat is not perfectly evenly spaced. The variation in R–R intervals (time between successive heartbeats) is HRV. Higher HRV — relative to your baseline — generally reflects greater parasympathetic (rest-and-digest) influence. Lower HRV relative to baseline often means sympathetic dominance: incomplete recovery, illness onset, alcohol, travel, or accumulated training stress.

Absolute numbers mislead. A resting overnight RMSSD of 45 ms might be excellent for one runner and suppressed for another. Age, fitness, genetics, and measurement method all shift the floor. Compare yourself to yourself over 7–14 days, not to a training partner’s Whoop score.

RMSSD vs SDNN: The Basics Runners Need

RMSSD (root mean square of successive differences) emphasizes short-term beat-to-beat variation and is heavily influenced by parasympathetic activity. Most consumer recovery scores lean on RMSSD or RMSSD-like metrics because they respond to overnight recovery status. Garmin’s overnight HRV Status and many morning finger-sensor protocols use RMSSD-family measures.

SDNN (standard deviation of NN intervals) reflects overall variability across a recording window — both sympathetic and parasympathetic influences. Longer recordings (24-hour Holter) make SDNN more meaningful; short overnight windows make RMSSD more common for readiness apps.

Practical takeaway: you do not need to choose RMSSD vs SDNN as a training philosophy. You need a consistent device, a stable measurement window (usually overnight), and a personal baseline. Switching from Apple + Athlytic to Garmin mid-block resets the comparison — treat the first 7–10 nights as calibration, not judgment.

Why Overnight Beats Spot Checks

Morning seated HRV with a chest strap can be excellent if you standardize posture, breathing, and timing. Most runners will not. Overnight wrist/optical HRV removes behavioral noise: you sleep, the watch samples, the algorithm averages a quiet window. Optical sensors are noisier than ECG chests straps, but consistency of method often beats occasional “perfect” measurements you forget to take.

Morning HRV Trends That Actually Matter

A single low morning is usually noise. Patterns are signal.

Useful Patterns

  • Downtrend over 3–5 days while training load rises — classic accumulated fatigue. Soften quality work before the crash.
  • Sharp drop (−1 SD or more) with elevated resting HR — often illness, alcohol, or severe sleep debt. See sleep and running performance for how sleep restriction shifts autonomic markers.
  • Rebound during a deload — HRV climbing back toward or above baseline confirms the recovery week worked.
  • Stable HRV with rising easy-run RPE — local muscle fatigue or life stress the watch underweights; trust the legs.

Numeric Example: Building a Baseline

Suppose your 14-day overnight RMSSD average is 62 ms with a standard deviation of 8 ms. Your normal band is roughly 54–70 ms (±1 SD). A morning reading of 58 ms is unremarkable. A reading of 50 ms (−1.5 SD) after two nights of 5.5 hours sleep and a late tempo is actionable context. A reading of 50 ms after a normal sleep week with no other symptoms is one data point — recheck tomorrow before rewriting the plan.

Pair HRV with resting HR. If HRV is −1.2 SD and resting HR is +6 bpm for three mornings, the autonomic picture is coherent: reduce intensity. If HRV is −1.2 SD but resting HR is normal and the warm-up jog feels snappy, consider measurement noise (watch fit, alcohol from one drink, menstrual phase, room heat).

Expert Note

Use a ±1 SD rule for hard sessions, not for easy volume. When overnight HRV is more than 1 standard deviation below your 14-day mean and resting HR is elevated or sleep was short, convert the day’s intervals/tempo to Zone 2 or rest. When HRV is low but warm-up RPE and resting HR are normal, run the easy day as planned and delay the call on quality until you have a second morning. One yellow icon should not erase a well-built training week — three aligned signals should.

The ±1 SD Rule: When to Skip Hard Sessions

Hard sessions (VO2 intervals, threshold, race-pace long runs) require a recovered autonomic system and willing legs. Easy aerobic volume is more forgiving and often helps recovery when kept truly easy — use the Zone 2 calculator so “easy” does not drift into junk Zone 3.

Decision Framework

  1. HRV within ±1 SD of 14-day mean: execute the plan. Do not add extra intensity because the score is green.
  2. HRV −1 to −1.5 SD, one morning: proceed if warm-up feels normal; reduce intensity 5–10% if warm-up RPE is high (e.g., 6×800 at 5K pace → 5×800 or pace +3–5 sec/km).
  3. HRV below −1 SD for 2+ mornings OR −1 SD plus resting HR +5 bpm: skip or replace hard work. Protect the stimulus for a better day.
  4. HRV above +1 SD after a recovery block: green light for planned quality — still not a license to stack an unplanned workout.

Worked Example

Baseline mean RMSSD = 55 ms, SD = 7 ms. Threshold: ~48 ms (−1 SD).

  • Monday: 49 ms, RHR normal, sleep 7.5 h → run planned tempo, maybe shave one rep if legs feel flat.
  • Tuesday–Wednesday: 44 ms and 42 ms, RHR +7 bpm, sleep 6 h → cancel Thursday intervals; two easy Zone 2 runs instead.
  • Friday: 52 ms, RHR back to baseline → resume quality Saturday if legs agree.

This is how HRV earns its keep: it catches the slide before you force a dead-legged interval session that digs a deeper hole — the pattern that precedes many cases of overtraining signs.

Brand Differences: Garmin, COROS, Apple Watch

All three estimate HRV from optical sensors (unless you add a chest strap). Algorithms and product framing differ.

Garmin

Garmin computes overnight HRV and displays HRV Status (balanced / unbalanced / low / poor) against a personal baseline, often alongside Body Battery and Training Status. Unbalanced status after heavy load is expected; chronic unbalanced with rising acute load is a warning. Interpret HRV alongside Garmin Training Status — Productive vs Overreaching is more useful when HRV, load, and VO2 estimate move together.

Strengths: native readiness language runners already see; long baselines on Forerunner/Fenix/Epix; integration with training load.

Limits: watch fit and alcohol swing readings; naps and travel nights muddy the overnight window; Status labels feel binary when physiology is continuous.

COROS

COROS EvoLab emphasizes recovery time, training load, and fitness trend more than a consumer “HRV score” brand identity, but overnight HR and recovery metrics still feed the model. Runners coming from Garmin often find COROS less chatty about daily green/yellow/red and more focused on planned recovery hours after hard sessions.

Strengths: clean training load narrative; excellent battery for multi-day camps where overnight wear is consistent.

Limits: less third-party HRV app ecosystem than Apple; you may need to export or track trends manually if you want explicit ±1 SD math.

Apple Watch

Apple records HRV (often SDNN in Health app context for spot samples) but does not ship a full runner-facing readiness suite. Apps like Athlytic, Bevel, or AutoSleep add RMSSD-style overnight scoring and recovery dashboards.

Strengths: frictionless Health data; strong sleep schedule tools that improve the sleep side of HRV (see sleep guide).

Limits: charging conflicts on non-Ultra models reduce overnight samples; optical accuracy varies; comparing Apple Health “HRV” charts directly to Garmin RMSSD baselines is apples-to-oranges without app mediation.

What Moves HRV Besides Training

  • Sleep duration and timing: two nights under 6.5 hours often suppress next-day HRV more than one hard workout.
  • Alcohol: even 1–2 drinks commonly crush overnight HRV and elevate RHR — your “mystery red readiness” after a celebration.
  • Illness: HRV frequently drops 24–48 hours before you feel sick. Respect multi-day drops.
  • Heat and dehydration: summer long runs raise overnight stress markers; easy run HR drifts — check with Zone 2 discipline.
  • Menstrual cycle: luteal phase can lower HRV and raise RHR; widen your personal “normal” band rather than panic.
  • Psychological stress: work deadlines suppress HRV without adding TSS — cut intensity, not necessarily all volume.

Integrating HRV With Training Decisions

HRV should modulate intensity at the margins of a periodized plan — not write the plan.

Green / Balanced + Planned Quality

Do the session as written. Cap the urge to “cash in” high readiness with bonus intervals.

Yellow / Slightly Low

Keep the session structure; reduce edge. Example: 5×3 min at VO2 pace with 2 min jog → 4×3 min or same reps at +2–3 sec/km. Maintain neuromuscular pattern without the deepest fatigue.

Red / Clearly Suppressed

Replace with Zone 2 or rest. If you absolutely must race (time trial league, championship), race — but do not stack a red-line workout the day after a red readiness cluster in training season.

Cross-Check Stack

Before skipping: HRV trend + resting HR + last night’s sleep + warm-up feeling. Three of four negative → rest. One of four negative → usually train. That stack is how you avoid both overtraining and chronic undertraining from readiness anxiety.

Common Mistakes

  • Switching devices weekly and treating every drop as overtraining
  • Skipping every yellow day until fitness stalls — then blaming the plan
  • Ignoring rising RHR because HRV “looked okay” once
  • Measuring after coffee, scrolling, or a stressful email — if you do morning spot checks, standardize ruthlessly
  • Using HRV to justify racing when legs are destroyed from unaccustomed downhill or strength DOMS — local fatigue ≠ systemic readiness

Final Takeaway

HRV is a trend tool for autonomic recovery, not a daily moral judgment. Learn RMSSD vs SDNN only enough to know your device’s language, build a 14-day baseline, and apply a ±1 SD rule to hard sessions when resting HR and sleep agree. Garmin, COROS, and Apple Watch all estimate; none replaces the warm-up jog. Pair HRV with resting heart rate, protect sleep, and read Garmin Training Status as a load narrative — not a single traffic light.

FAQ

What is a good HRV for a runner?

There is no universal good number. A 30-year-old may sit near 60–80 ms RMSSD overnight while a 50-year-old masters runner may be healthy at 30–45 ms. Good means stable relative to your 14-day baseline, with rebounds after recovery weeks. Chase trends and training consistency, not a friend’s Whoop average.

Should I skip a workout when Garmin HRV Status is unbalanced?

Not automatically. One unbalanced morning after a hard long run is expected. Unbalanced for 2–3 days with elevated resting HR, short sleep, or heavy legs — yes, swap quality for Zone 2 or rest. Use the ±1 SD idea plus context; do not let a single status label delete a key session if warm-up feels normal and metrics are only mildly off.

Is Apple Watch HRV comparable to Garmin HRV?

Not directly. Sampling windows, metrics (SDNN vs RMSSD-style overnight averages), and third-party scoring differ. Pick one ecosystem for decisions. If you switch, recalibrate for 7–10 nights before applying rest rules.

Can HRV detect overtraining?

HRV can flag accumulating non-functional overreaching when it trends down for a week-plus alongside rising RHR, poor sleep, and performance decay. It cannot diagnose clinical overtraining syndrome alone. Combine metrics with mood, illness, and workout data — see overtraining signs for runners.
