Tapering Before a Race: Volume, Intensity, and Why Legs Feel Heavy

Updated August 2026 · 14 min read

You have spent sixteen weeks building fitness. Now your coach — or your plan — says to run less. Your legs feel sluggish on easy jogs. You are convinced you are losing fitness by the hour. You add a "sharpener" workout. You arrive at the start line tired, doubting, and overtrained for race day instead of tapered.

Tapering is the most psychologically difficult phase of training because it feels counterintuitive. Reduced training produces better race performance — not despite the rest, but because of it. The physiology is well-documented: fatigue dissipates faster than fitness, glycogen supercompensates, and neuromuscular function sharpens with maintained intensity and cut volume.

This guide covers 2–3 week taper structures by race distance, the maintain-intensity-cut-volume principle, why heavy legs are normal, glycogen supercompensation, and how to execute without panic-training your way out of peak form.

What Tapering Actually Does

Training creates two parallel trajectories: fitness (slow to build, slow to lose) and fatigue (fast to build, fast to lose). Peak race performance occurs when fitness remains high and fatigue drops — the gap between them maximized. Taper reduces training load so fatigue falls while fitness holds.

Documented taper benefits from meta-analyses and controlled studies:

  • Performance improvement: 2–3% on average across endurance events — meaningful at every distance
  • Reduced muscle damage markers: creatine kinase and perceived soreness drop
  • Glycogen supercompensation: muscle glycogen stores rise above baseline with reduced training plus adequate carbs
  • Neuromuscular freshness: maintained through short high-intensity touches despite volume cut
  • Psychological readiness: rest and reduced decision fatigue improve race-day focus

Taper does not add fitness. It reveals fitness already built. The mistake is treating taper as lost training days instead of invested recovery.

Maintain intensity, cut volume

The cardinal taper rule: reduce duration and frequency of easy miles; preserve short race-pace or faster segments. Eliminating all intensity produces "flat" legs; maintaining volume produces accumulated fatigue. Cut 40–60% volume while keeping 2–4 neuromuscular reminders per week.

Taper Length by Race Distance: 2–3 Weeks

Taper duration scales with race distance and training load. Longer events require longer fatigue dissipation; shorter events need less total reduction but still benefit from structured rest.

5K: 7–10 Days

5K performance depends on VO2 max and neuromuscular speed — qualities that decay slowly. A 7–10 day taper with 30–50% volume reduction suffices.

  • Week minus 1: 50% volume; one session of 3–4 × 3 min at 5K pace or faster
  • Race week: 40–50% volume; strides and one short race-pace touch (see Race Week Preparation Guide)

10K: 10–14 Days

10K sits between speed and endurance taper needs. Two-week taper is standard for most training volumes.

  • Week minus 2: 70% volume; last moderate workout — e.g., 3 × 1 mile at 10K pace
  • Week minus 1: 40–50% volume; 2–3 × 2 min at race pace mid-week; strides only otherwise

Half Marathon: 14 Days (2 Weeks)

Half marathon taper is the most studied protocol — typically 2 weeks with progressive volume reduction.

  • Week minus 2: 75–80% volume; last long run 8–10 miles; one tempo or race-pace session
  • Week minus 1: 40–50% volume; short race-pace segments; carb emphasis final 3 days

Pacing execution on race day depends on taper quality — see Half Marathon Pacing Strategy for split planning once freshness is banked.

Marathon: 14–21 Days (2–3 Weeks)

Marathon accumulates the most training fatigue. Three-week tapers are common for high-mileage plans; two-week tapers work for moderate volume.

  • Week minus 3 (optional): 85% volume; last big effort — marathon-pace long run segment or final long run of 18–20 miles
  • Week minus 2: 70% volume; no run over 10 miles; one moderate marathon-pace session
  • Week minus 1: 40–50% volume; 2–3 short easy runs with strides; carb load days 3–1

Marathon taper anxiety peaks because mileage drops dramatically — trust the process. Fitness built over months does not evaporate in twenty days.

Volume Reduction: How Much and How Fast

Optimal taper reduces training volume 41–60% over the taper period without changing training frequency dramatically. Two models:

Linear taper

Steady reduction each week — e.g., 80% → 60% → 40% of peak over three weeks. Simple, predictable, works for most amateur plans.

Exponential taper

Gradual reduction early, sharp cut in final 5–7 days. Some research suggests exponential tapers outperform linear for events over 90 minutes — more rest concentrated when it matters most.

Intensity Maintenance: What to Keep

Volume and intensity taper differently. Cut volume aggressively; cut intensity minimally.

Keep during taper:

  • Strides: 4–6 × 15–20 sec fast relaxed pickups, 2–3× per week — maintains leg turnover without fatigue
  • Race-pace touches: short segments at goal pace — 2–3 × 2 min for 10K, 2–3 × 1 mile for half, 2 × 10 min marathon pace for marathon
  • Easy run effort: truly easy — if HR or pace creeps up, you are running too hard on "recovery" days

Eliminate during taper:

  • Long runs over race-distance-appropriate limits (10 mi for half taper week, 6 mi for marathon taper week)
  • VO2 max intervals, heavy hill repeats, strength PR attempts
  • Back-to-back hard days — every run should leave you feeling better than you started

The "one last hard workout" impulse — 5–7 days before a race — is the most common taper sabotage. Trust the training block. A short race-pace reminder is not a fitness test.

Why Legs Feel Heavy During Taper

Taper heaviness is nearly universal and widely misinterpreted as detraining. It is not.

Mechanisms:

  • Glycogen and water retention: as training stress drops, muscles store extra glycogen; each gram of glycogen binds ~3g water — legs feel stiff and heavy
  • Reduced blood flow habituation: daily high-volume training maintains perfusion; sudden reduction changes sensation without fitness loss
  • Neuromuscular anticipation: psychological stress plus reduced movement creates perceived sluggishness
  • Micro-recovery inflammation: repairing cumulative micro-damage from peak block — part of the healing that taper enables

Heavy legs at easy pace during taper week correlate with better race performance in observational athlete reports — not worse. Race morning adrenaline, warm-up, and first mile cadence typically transform heavy into responsive within 10–15 minutes.

Do not:

  • Add miles to "test" legs
  • Interpret heavy strides as need for a hard workout
  • Weigh yourself daily and panic about 2–4 lb gain (glycogen water — beneficial)

Glycogen Supercompensation

Muscle glycogen is the primary fuel for race efforts at marathon intensity and a significant contributor for half marathon and 10K. Normal training depletes and replenishes glycogen cyclically. Taper creates supercompensation — stores rise 10–20% above habitual levels when training load drops and carbohydrate intake is adequate.

Requirements for supercompensation:

  1. Reduced training load: final 7–14 days volume cut — depletion from training stops
  2. Adequate carbohydrate intake: 8–10 g/kg/day in final 3 days for half and marathon; 5–7 g/kg sufficient for 5K/10K
  3. Time: glycogen stores take 48–72 hours to maximally replenish and supercompensate — not one pre-race meal

Old depletion-then-load protocols (exhausting runs followed by carb binge) are obsolete — modern approach is gradual load without depletion phase. Excessive depletion adds fatigue without superior glycogen stores.

Supercompensation benefits events over 60–75 minutes most. A 5K relies more on phosphocreatine and anaerobic contribution — glycogen loading is marginal. Marathon performance is directly glycogen-limited for most amateurs — supercompensation matters enormously.

Taper Mistakes That Cost Race Day

  • Over-tapering: cutting volume 80%+ and eliminating all intensity — legs feel flat, neuromuscular system under-stimulated
  • Under-tapering: running peak mileage until 3 days out — fatigue never clears
  • Panic workouts: hard sessions 4–7 days pre-race to "prove" fitness
  • Travel and standing: expo walking, sightseeing, late flights — fatigue without training log entry
  • Illness exposure: taper immune window is slightly vulnerable; prioritize sleep and hand hygiene
  • Complete rest: 5 days of zero running before a marathon produces stale legs for many athletes — short easy jogs maintain feel

Sample Two-Week Half Marathon Taper

Peak week: 45 miles, long run 12 miles, one tempo.

Week -2 (34 miles, 75%):

  • Mon: rest or 4 mi easy
  • Tue: 6 mi with 3 × 1 mi at HM pace
  • Wed: 5 mi easy + strides
  • Thu: 5 mi easy
  • Fri: rest
  • Sat: 8 mi easy — last "long" run
  • Sun: 6 mi easy

Week -1 (20 miles, 44%):

  • Mon: rest
  • Tue: 5 mi easy + 4 strides
  • Wed: 4 mi with 2 × 2 min at HM pace
  • Thu: 4 mi easy; carb emphasis begins
  • Fri: 3 mi easy + 4 strides
  • Sat: 2 mi jog + 3 strides
  • Sun: race

Connecting Taper to Race Week

Taper is the macro strategy; race week is the micro execution. The final seven days layer nutrition timing, sleep banking, gear prep, and anxiety management on top of volume reduction already underway.

Final Takeaway

Tapering before a race is a performance intervention, not a training gap. Cut volume 40–60% over 2–3 weeks depending on distance; maintain intensity through strides and short race-pace segments; expect heavy legs and trust them; load carbohydrates progressively for glycogen supercompensation on long events. Fitness built over months survives a proper taper intact — fatigue does not. Arrive at the start line rested, glycogen-loaded, and neurologically sharp. That is the point of every mile you already ran.

FAQ

How long should I taper before a race?

5K: 7–10 days. 10K: 10–14 days. Half marathon: 14 days. Marathon: 14–21 days (2–3 weeks). Longer events require longer tapers because accumulated fatigue is greater. Reduce weekly volume 40–60% over the taper period while maintaining short race-pace segments.

Should I reduce intensity or volume during taper?

Reduce volume primarily — cut 40–60% of weekly mileage. Maintain intensity through strides (4–6 × 15–20 sec, 2–3× weekly) and short race-pace touches (e.g., 2–3 × 2 min at goal pace). Eliminate long runs, VO2 max intervals, and hard strength work. Cutting both volume and intensity produces flat, stale legs on race day.

Why do my legs feel heavy and slow during taper?

Normal taper physiology. Glycogen supercompensation stores extra water in muscles, creating stiffness. Reduced daily training changes blood flow sensation. Micro-recovery from peak training block continues. The heaviness typically resolves within the first mile on race day. Do not add workouts to "test" legs — trust the process.

Does carb loading during taper improve performance?

Yes, for events over 60–75 minutes. Reduced training load plus 8–10 g carbs/kg/day in the final 3 days produces glycogen supercompensation — stores 10–20% above baseline. For 5K and 10K, normal carb-forward meals suffice. Loading takes 48–72 hours; one pre-race pasta dinner alone is insufficient.
