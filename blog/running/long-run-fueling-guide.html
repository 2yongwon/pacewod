Long Run Fueling: Carbs, Electrolytes, and GI Strategy for 90+ Minute Runs

Updated August 2026 · 12 min read

At mile 14 of an 18-mile long run, your legs feel fine but your pace starts slipping. Heart rate climbs at the same effort. The watch says you are fading — and the real culprit is not fitness. Your glycogen stores ran low 30 minutes ago because you skipped mid-run fuel, assuming breakfast would carry you through.

Runs under 75 minutes rarely require mid-run nutrition for most trained athletes. Once you cross 90 minutes — the threshold where liver and muscle glycogen depletion becomes performance-limiting — what you eat and drink during the run matters as much as the pace on your plan. The same logic applies on race day for half marathons and marathons.

This guide covers carbohydrate targets per hour, electrolyte strategy, practical fuel formats, and how to train your gut so race-day fueling feels routine instead of experimental.

Why 90 Minutes Is the Fueling Threshold

The average trained runner stores roughly 1,500–2,000 calories of glycogen in muscles and liver — enough to sustain moderate effort for 90–120 minutes before performance drops. Once glycogen falls below critical levels, pace at a given effort rises, perceived exertion spikes, and the final miles of a long run become a survival shuffle instead of aerobic training.

Exogenous carbohydrates — gels, chews, sports drink — spare glycogen by providing an alternative fuel source absorbed from the gut. Even 30–60 grams of carbs per hour extends time to exhaustion and keeps pace stable through the back half of a long session. Skipping fuel on a 2-hour run is not toughness; it is leaving aerobic adaptation on the table because the last 30 minutes were glycogen-depleted junk miles.

Heat, intensity, and training status accelerate glycogen burn. A 90-minute easy run in cool weather may survive without fuel for some athletes. A 90-minute run with marathon-pace segments in August will not. Default to fueling for any run expected to exceed 75 minutes when in doubt.

Train the gut like you train pace

Start practicing race-day carb intake on long runs eight to twelve weeks before your goal event. The gut adapts to higher carb volumes with repeated exposure — do not wait until race morning to discover 60 g/hr causes cramps.

Carbohydrate Targets: How Much Per Hour

Sports nutrition research consistently supports 30–60 grams of carbohydrate per hour for endurance efforts lasting 1–2.5 hours. For runs approaching 3 hours or marathon race pace, 60–90 grams per hour is optimal for athletes who have trained their gut to tolerate it.

Starting points by run duration

  • 75–90 minutes: 20–30 g/hr — one gel or a few chews is enough to start
  • 90–120 minutes: 30–45 g/hr — gel every 30–40 minutes or diluted sports drink
  • 120–180 minutes (marathon long runs): 45–60 g/hr — build toward race-day target over several weeks
  • Marathon race pace efforts: 60–90 g/hr for experienced fuelers; requires gut training

Increase carb intake gradually. Jumping from zero mid-run fuel to 60 g/hr in one session invites GI disaster. Add 10–15 g/hr every two to three long runs until you reach your target.

Carb formats that work on the run

  • Energy gels: 20–25 g carbs each; compact, fast absorption; chase with water
  • Chews and blocks: 20–30 g per serving; easier on some stomachs than concentrated gels
  • Sports drink: 14–20 g carbs per 8 oz depending on concentration; provides fluid and electrolytes simultaneously
  • Real food: bananas, dates, pretzels — work for easy long runs but impractical at marathon pace

Most runners combine formats: sports drink for baseline hydration plus a gel every 30–40 minutes for additional carb density. Use the Running Calories Calculator to estimate total energy expenditure, then ensure mid-run carbs cover a meaningful fraction of the burn.

Timing: When to Start and How Often

Begin fueling early — within the first 30–45 minutes — rather than waiting until you feel hungry or tired. By the time perceived hunger or pace fade appears, glycogen is already depleted and you are playing catch-up.

A practical schedule for a 2-hour long run targeting 45 g/hr:

  1. Minute 0–15: Pre-run breakfast 2–3 hours before (see pre-run section below). Sip water.
  2. Minute 30: First gel (25 g) with 4–6 oz water.
  3. Minute 60: Second gel (25 g) or chew serving. Start electrolyte drink if sweating heavily.
  4. Minute 90: Third gel or drink top-up. Assess feel — adjust final 30 minutes if GI is unsettled.

Set a watch alert every 30 minutes as a fuel reminder. Racing and long runs compress time perception — without alerts, athletes routinely forget to eat until mile 16.

Pre-Run Nutrition: Setting Up the Long Run

Mid-run fueling does not replace a solid pre-run meal. Eat 2–3 hours before start time to top off glycogen without causing stomach distress during the first hour.

  • Target: 1–3 g/kg body weight of easily digestible carbs — oatmeal, toast with honey, banana, white rice
  • Protein and fat: keep low pre-run; they slow gastric emptying
  • Hydration: 16–20 oz fluid in the 2–3 hours before; pale yellow urine is the target
  • Caffeine (optional): 3–6 mg/kg taken 45–60 minutes pre-run can reduce perceived effort; many gels include caffeine for mid-run dosing

Runs starting before dawn may require a smaller, closer pre-run snack — half a banana and a gel at the door — because a full breakfast is impractical at 5 AM. Adjust mid-run fuel upward to compensate for the lighter pre-load.

Electrolytes: Sodium, Sweat, and Heat

Carbohydrates get most of the attention, but sodium loss through sweat drives cramping, hyponatremia risk, and fluid retention problems on long hot runs. Individual sweat sodium concentration varies from 300 to 1,500+ mg per liter of sweat — a range that makes one-size-fits-all advice unreliable.

Practical sodium guidelines

  • Cool weather, <90 minutes: water alone may suffice if pre-run sodium intake was normal
  • Moderate heat or 90–150 minutes: 300–600 mg sodium per hour via sports drink or salt capsules
  • Hot conditions or heavy sweaters: 600–1,000 mg sodium per hour; consider sweat testing or white-salt-residue assessment on gear

Potassium and magnesium matter less acutely during the run itself but support recovery afterward. A balanced electrolyte drink covers baseline needs for most athletes. Heavy sweaters and hot-weather marathoners benefit from dedicated sodium supplementation beyond what standard sports drinks provide — many contain only 100–200 mg sodium per serving.

Weigh yourself before and after a long run (dry clothes both times). Each pound lost is roughly 16 oz of fluid deficit. Replacing 150% of sweat losses over the 4–6 hours post-run supports recovery — water alone without sodium reduces retention efficiency.

Fluid Intake: How Much to Drink

Over-drinking plain water without sodium is as dangerous as under-drinking in long events — it dilutes blood sodium and causes exercise-associated hyponatremia. Drink to thirst as a starting heuristic, adjusted for heat and sweat rate.

  • Cool conditions: 12–16 oz per hour
  • Moderate heat: 16–24 oz per hour
  • Hot and humid: up to 28 oz per hour for heavy sweaters, paired with sodium

Carry fluid or plan loops past water fountains for any run over 90 minutes without aid stations. Dehydration of 2% body weight impairs performance and elevates heart rate at the same pace — the cardiac drift you see on your watch may partly reflect fluid deficit, not just fitness.

GI Strategy: Avoiding Stomach Problems

Gastrointestinal distress is the number-one reason runners DNF or abandon fueling plans. The gut receives reduced blood flow during intense exercise — eating too much, too fast, or trying new products on race day overwhelms a system already under stress.

Gut-training protocol

  1. Practice exact race-day products on long runs, not just easy days
  2. Increase carb rate by 10–15 g/hr every 2–3 weeks
  3. Test gels at goal marathon pace, not only recovery pace — GI tolerance drops at intensity
  4. Always chase gels with 4–8 oz water; concentrated carb boluses without fluid delay absorption and cause cramping
  5. Log GI response after each long run — product, timing, pace, weather — and eliminate repeat offenders

When GI distress hits mid-run

Slow pace for 5–10 minutes and sip water. Avoid stacking another gel on an unsettled stomach. Reduce carb intake temporarily and finish the run if symptoms are mild. Stop if vomiting, sharp pain, or dizziness appear. Most GI issues on long runs trace to too much too soon, new untested products, or insufficient water with gels — all preventable with rehearsal.

Fueling by Race Distance

Half marathon long runs (90–105 minutes)

Target 30–45 g/hr. One gel at 30 and 60 minutes plus sports drink covers most athletes. Practice at goal half marathon pace during the final 20 minutes of at least two long runs.

Marathon long runs (120–150 minutes)

Target 45–60 g/hr, building toward 60–90 g/hr if racing competitively. Simulate aid-station rhythm — gel every 30 minutes, drink at every opportunity. The last 30 minutes at marathon pace on depleted-but-fueled legs teaches race-specific fatigue management.

Ultra and trail (3+ hours)

Lower intensity allows real food — sandwiches, potatoes, broth. Carb targets remain 30–60 g/hr minimum; palatability and variety matter more over 5+ hours. Sodium needs often exceed marathon defaults due to duration and terrain.

Common Fueling Mistakes

  • Waiting too long to start: fuel early, not when pace fades
  • Trying new products on race day: every gel, drink, and chew should be tested on long runs
  • Under-fueling to save calories for weight loss: undermining long-run quality to cut intake is counterproductive — see our Running for Weight Loss guide for better approaches
  • Ignoring electrolytes in heat: water alone without sodium in hot long runs risks hyponatremia and cramps
  • Skipping fuel on "easy" long runs: gut training requires rehearsal at all paces, not just race-pace sessions

Putting It Together: Sample 2-Hour Long Run Plan

For a 150-pound runner targeting 45 g/hr on a moderate-temperature morning:

  • Pre-run (2.5 hr before): Oatmeal with banana and honey (~60 g carbs), 16 oz water
  • Minute 30: Gel #1 (25 g) + 6 oz water
  • Minute 55: Gel #2 (25 g) + electrolyte drink (12 oz, ~200 mg sodium)
  • Minute 80: Gel #3 (25 g) + 6 oz water
  • Post-run (within 30 min): Recovery shake or meal with 3:1 carb-to-protein ratio, 20 oz fluid with sodium

Adjust gel count and fluid for duration, heat, and personal tolerance. Pair pacing with the Pace Calculator and follow structured long-run progression in our Long Run Guide and Half Marathon Training Plan.

Final Takeaway

Long runs over 90 minutes require deliberate carbohydrate and fluid intake — 30–60 g of carbs per hour for most athletes, scaling up with duration and intensity. Electrolytes, especially sodium, matter in heat and for heavy sweaters. The gut adapts with practice; race-day fueling should feel boring because you have rehearsed it for months.

Start fueling early, increase gradually, test everything at goal pace, and log what works. The long run builds legs and lungs — mid-run nutrition ensures the last hour is training, not survival.

FAQ

Do I need to fuel on runs under 90 minutes?

Most trained runners do not need mid-run carbs for sessions under 75–90 minutes at easy to moderate intensity, provided they ate adequately beforehand. Exceptions include fasted runs (where even 20 g/hr may help), hot conditions accelerating glycogen burn, and runs with high-intensity segments. When in doubt on an 80-minute run, one gel at 40 minutes is low-risk insurance.

What is the best gel or sports drink for long runs?

The best product is the one your stomach tolerates at goal pace after repeated testing. Popular options — Maurten, GU, Huma, Tailwind, Gatorade Endurance — differ in carb type, osmolality, and sodium content, but individual tolerance dominates brand performance. Test two or three options across four to six long runs and eliminate anything that causes cramping, nausea, or urgency.

How do I avoid hitting the wall on long runs?

"The wall" is glycogen depletion — pace and cognition crash when stored carbs run out. Prevent it by eating 30–60 g of carbs per hour starting at 30 minutes, pacing the early miles conservatively, and arriving at the start with topped-up glycogen from a pre-run meal. Cardiac drift and rising RPE at stable pace in the final third often signal under-fueling rather than under-fitness.

Can I fuel with real food instead of gels?

Real food works on easy long runs and ultras where pace is moderate and chewing is practical — bananas, dates, pretzels, boiled potatoes. At half marathon and marathon pace, gels and chews are preferred because they require no chewing, absorb faster, and deliver precise carb doses. Many runners use real food for training variety and gels for race-day intensity.
