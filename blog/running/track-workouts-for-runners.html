Track Training

Track Workouts for Runners: 400m, 800m, and Mile Repeats on the Oval

Updated August 2026 · 12 min read

The track is the most honest surface in running. Four hundred meters per lap, marked in 100m increments, no hills, no traffic, no GPS drift around corners. When your watch says 76 seconds for a 400m repeat, you can look at the clock on the infield and know whether the watch or your legs lied.

That precision is why track workouts remain the gold standard for interval training — despite the intimidation factor of showing up to an oval where collegiate sprinters are flying lane 1 and the high school team occupies the back straight. Once you understand lane etiquette, standard distances, and how to structure sessions with appropriate rest, the track becomes the most efficient place to build speed, VO2 max, and race-specific fitness.

This guide covers track basics, three complete workout sessions (400m, 800m, and mile repeats), rest interval guidelines, and when to trust GPS versus measured splits. For broader interval methodology and pace targets, see our Interval Training for Runners guide. Use the Pace Chart to convert repeat times to race-equivalent paces, and the 5K Training Plan for integrating track work into a full program.

Why Train on the Track

Road intervals introduce variables — elevation, surface, turns, traffic — that obscure whether pace changes reflect fitness or terrain. The track removes those variables. Every rep covers the same distance on the same surface with the same turn radius (in a given lane). Split times are comparable rep to rep, session to session, month to month.

  • Measured distance: No GPS calibration needed; 400m is 400m
  • Flat surface: Pace changes reflect effort, not hills
  • Controlled environment: Predictable footing, no stoplights, no cyclists
  • Split feedback: 100m marks allow mid-rep pace checks on longer intervals
  • Repeatability: Same venue, same lanes, same conditions for retesting

The track is not just for elite athletes. Recreational runners preparing for a 5K, 10K, or half marathon benefit from one track session every 7–14 days during intensity phases — the measured feedback accelerates pacing skill that road running alone cannot provide.

Track Distances and Lane Math

A standard outdoor track is 400 meters per lap when measured on the inside lane (lane 1). The staggered start lines painted on the track account for the extra distance in outer lanes — critical for races, less critical for training reps where you pick a lane and stay in it.

Standard Track Distances

  • 100m: One straightaway (start line to first curve)
  • 200m: Half lap — one straight + one curve, or curve + straight
  • 400m: One full lap — the fundamental track repeat distance
  • 800m: Two laps
  • 1200m: Three laps
  • 1600m (mile): Four laps — slightly short of a true mile (1609m) but standard for track work
  • 3200m (two mile): Eight laps

Lane Distance Add-On

Each lane outward adds roughly 7–8 meters per lap. Training in lane 4 instead of lane 1 adds approximately 22–28 meters per 400m rep — enough to throw off pace calculations if you switch lanes between sessions. Pick a lane, note it in your log, and stay consistent.

Practical rule: Use lane 1 or 2 when the track is empty. Move to lane 3–5 only when faster groups are using inner lanes. Never train in lane 6+ unless the track is otherwise full — the extra distance makes split comparison unreliable.

Indoor vs Outdoor Tracks

Indoor tracks vary: 200m (most common in the US), 300m, or 400m. A 200m indoor track requires 10 laps for a mile repeat — mentally different from 4 laps outdoors. Check the facility's lap distance before planning workouts. Banked indoor tracks run faster; adjust pace expectations accordingly.

Expert Note

Always confirm whether your track is marked in meters or yards. Older US tracks may be 440 yards per lap (slightly longer than 400m). Running 4 × 400m on a 440-yard track means each rep is ~402m — negligible for one session, but it compounds over a training block if you log "400m" times that are actually 402m efforts.

Track Etiquette and Lane Rules

Track etiquette keeps sessions safe and respectful when multiple groups share the oval. Most conflicts come from runners in the wrong lane or unaware of faster athletes approaching from behind.

Lane Hierarchy

  • Lane 1 (innermost): Reserved for the fastest athletes during workouts — sprinters, athletes on hard reps. If you are doing easy recovery jogs between reps, move outward.
  • Lanes 2–3: Standard training lanes for distance runners doing interval work
  • Lanes 4–6: Recovery jogging, warm-up laps, walkers, and athletes between reps
  • Outside lanes / infield: Warm-up, cool-down, drills, and standing rest between reps

Rules That Prevent Collisions

  1. Run counterclockwise. Every track runs counterclockwise unless posted otherwise. Running clockwise into oncoming traffic is the most common track faux pas.
  2. Never stop in lane 1. If you need to stop — walk, cramp, tie a shoe — move to the infield or outer lanes first.
  3. Look before entering. Enter the track from the infield during a gap in traffic. Do not step onto lane 1 without checking for approaching runners.
  4. Pass on the right. Faster runners pass on the right (outside). Slower runners stay left (inside of their lane) or move to an outer lane when aware of faster traffic.
  5. No headphones during shared sessions. You need to hear "Track!" shouted by approaching runners. One earbud at most.
  6. Yield to organized teams. If a college or high school team is running a structured workout, use outer lanes or ask the coach which lanes are available. Do not weave through a team's lane 1 sprints.
  7. Do not use starting blocks unless you are a registered athlete with coach permission — blocks damage the track surface.

When to Ask Permission

Public school tracks are often open outside school hours — check local rules. University tracks may require a pass or restrict public access during team practice (typically 3–6 PM). Community tracks at parks are usually open dawn to dusk. If a team is practicing, ask the coach: "Which lanes can I use?" — most coaches appreciate the question and will assign lanes 4–6.

Workout Session Structure

Every track session follows the same skeleton: warm-up, drills (optional), main set, cool-down. Skipping warm-up on the track is more dangerous than on roads — cold hamstrings and hip flexors on a surface designed for speed produce strains.

Standard Session Template

  • Warm-up10–20 min easy jog (2–3 laps) + dynamic drills
  • Drills4 × 80m strides (optional but recommended)
  • Main setPrescribed repeats with rest intervals
  • Cool-down10–15 min easy jog (2–3 laps)

Total session time: 45–75 minutes depending on main set volume. Track sessions are quality work — schedule them on days with no other intensity and at least 48 hours from your long run.

400m Repeat Workouts

400m repeats are the bread and butter of track training. They develop VO2 max, speed endurance, and the ability to maintain pace under lactate accumulation. Distance varies by goal race and fitness level.

Session 1: VO2 Max 400s

Target: 5K to 10K racers building top-end aerobic power

  • Warm-up15 min easy + 4 × 80m strides
  • Main set8–12 × 400m at 5K race pace
  • Rest200m jog (60–90 sec) between reps
  • Cool-down10–15 min easy

Pace Targets (400m Splits)

  • 25:00 5K runner (8:03/mi): 400m reps at 1:58–2:02
  • 22:00 5K runner (7:04/mi): 400m reps at 1:43–1:47
  • 20:00 5K runner (6:26/mi): 400m reps at 1:34–1:38
  • 45:00 10K runner (7:15/mi): 400m reps at 1:46–1:50

Use the Pace Chart to find your race-pace 400m split from your current 5K or 10K time. Reps should feel hard (RPE 8–9) but sustainable — if rep 8 is more than 5 seconds slower than rep 1, you started too fast.

Session 2: Speed Endurance 400s

Target: 5K racers sharpening race speed; 800m–mile specialists

  • Main set6–8 × 400m at mile race pace or faster
  • Rest400m jog (2–3 min) between reps — full recovery

Mile race pace 400s with full recovery develop raw speed. Example: 6:00 miler runs 400m reps at 1:28–1:32 with 400m jog rest. Fewer reps, higher quality, longer rest — this is neuromuscular work, not VO2 max accumulation.

400m Pacing Tips

  • Even or negative splits within the rep: First 200m should match or be 1 sec slower than second 200m — not the reverse
  • Check the 200m mark: At halfway, you should be at half your target time ± 1 second
  • Rep count consistency: All reps within 2–3 seconds of each other indicates good pacing; a 10-second spread means rep 1 was too fast

800m Repeat Workouts

800m repeats bridge the gap between speed and endurance — long enough to accumulate lactate, short enough to maintain quality across 4–6 reps. They are the primary workout for 5K and 10K preparation.

Session 3: Threshold 800s

Target: 10K to half marathon racers building lactate threshold

  • Warm-up15–20 min easy + 4 × 80m strides
  • Main set4–6 × 800m at 10K race pace
  • Rest400m jog (2–3 min) between reps
  • Cool-down10–15 min easy

Pace Targets (800m Splits)

  • 25:00 5K / ~52:00 10K: 800m at 4:06–4:10
  • 22:00 5K / ~45:30 10K: 800m at 3:36–3:40
  • 50:00 10K: 800m at 3:58–4:02
  • 1:45 half marathon: 800m at 3:58–4:02 (10K pace range)

Session 4: VO2 Max 800s

Target: 5K racers in peak phase

  • Main set5 × 800m at 5K race pace minus 2–3 sec per 400m
  • Rest200m jog (90 sec – 2 min) between reps

These are harder than threshold 800s — shorter rest, faster pace. A 22:00 5K runner (7:04/mi) targets 3:28–3:32 per 800m with 200m jog recovery. If you cannot hold pace by rep 4, the pace is too aggressive.

800m Pacing Tips

  • First lap controlled: 800m rep 1 lap 1 should feel slightly easy — the pain comes in lap 2
  • 400m split check: At the start line after lap 1, check your 400m time against target
  • Finish strong: Last 200m of each 800m rep should be equal or faster than the first 200m — positive split within the rep is acceptable on 800s if the overall time hits target

Mile Repeat Workouts

Mile repeats (1600m) are the longest standard track interval — they develop race-specific endurance for 5K and 10K and serve as fitness benchmarks when repeated monthly.

Session 5: Race-Pace Mile Repeats

Target: 5K and 10K racers in specific preparation phase

  • Warm-up20 min easy + 4 × 80m strides
  • Main set3–5 × 1600m (mile) at 5K or 10K race pace
  • Rest400m jog (3–4 min) between reps
  • Cool-down10–15 min easy

Pace Targets (Mile Splits)

  • 25:00 5K runner: Mile reps at 7:58–8:02 (5K pace)
  • 22:00 5K runner: Mile reps at 6:58–7:02
  • 50:00 10K runner: Mile reps at 7:58–8:02 (10K pace)
  • 45:00 10K runner: Mile reps at 7:13–7:17

Five mile repeats at 5K race pace is a demanding session — appropriate 4–6 weeks before a goal 5K. Three mile repeats at 10K race pace is standard 10K preparation work. See our 5K Training Plan for where mile repeats fit in a periodized schedule.

Session 6: Cut-Down Miles

Target: Advanced athletes sharpening for race day

  • Main set3 × 1600m — rep 1 at 10K pace, rep 2 at 5K pace, rep 3 at 5K pace minus 5 sec/mi
  • Rest400m jog (3 min) between reps

Cut-down miles teach progressive effort — the fitness to accelerate when fatigued. Rep 3 should feel like the last mile of a 5K race. Reserve for athletes who have completed at least 4 weeks of standard mile repeat work first.

Expert Note

Log track workouts by rep times, not average GPS pace. A 6:00 mile repeat is 6:00 — your watch may report 5:55 or 6:08 depending on lane, GPS sampling, and where you start/stop the lap button. Write down split times from the track clock or a manual lap trigger at the start line each rep.

Rest Intervals: How Much Recovery Between Reps

Rest duration determines which energy system the workout targets. Too little rest turns VO2 max intervals into a tempo run; too much rest turns threshold work into sprints with no cumulative fatigue.

Rest Guidelines by Workout Type

  • VO2 max (short rest): Equal-distance jog recovery — 200m jog after 400m rep (~60–90 sec), 400m jog after 800m rep (~2–3 min). HR should reach 65–75% max before starting the next rep.
  • Threshold (moderate rest): 50–75% of rep duration — 2–3 min after 800m, 3–4 min after mile. HR should reach 60–70% max.
  • Speed / neuromuscular (full rest): 100% of rep duration or greater — 3–4 min after 400m at mile pace. HR should drop below 65% max. Full recovery preserves rep quality.
  • Race simulation (minimal rest): 30–60 sec after 400m at 5K pace — advanced only, mimics race fatigue. Use sparingly (once per training block).

Active vs Standing Rest

Active recovery (jog): Standard for distance runners. Maintains blood flow, clears lactate faster than standing, keeps muscles warm. Jog the rest distance at 50–60% effort — not a walk, not a shuffle that becomes a second workout.

Standing/walking rest: Acceptable for speed-focused 400m work at mile pace or faster. Also appropriate if active recovery jog heart rate does not drop below 75% max before the next rep — walk until HR recovers.

When to Cut a Session Short

Stop the main set if:

  • Two consecutive reps miss target pace by more than 5 seconds (400m) or 10 seconds (800m/mile)
  • Form breaks down — heel strike increases, cadence drops, upper body rotation appears
  • Heart rate exceeds 95% max for more than 30 seconds during a rep and does not recover below 75% within the rest interval
  • Sharp pain (not discomfort) appears in calves, hamstrings, or shins

Cutting a session from 8 reps to 6 is not failure — it is pacing data. Log what happened and adjust next session.

GPS vs Measured Splits on the Track

GPS watches are unreliable on tracks. Understanding why prevents the frustration of "running 6:00 mile pace" on your watch while the track clock shows 6:15.

Why GPS Fails on the Oval

  • Curve sampling: GPS records straight-line segments between sampling points; curves are cut short — reported distance is less than actual lap distance
  • Lane position: GPS does not know which lane you are in; inner lane vs outer lane varies reported distance by 5–10m per lap
  • Multi-path interference: Stadium structures, bleachers, and fences bounce GPS signals
  • Lap button timing: Starting the lap 5m before the line and stopping 5m after adds error to split calculations

Typical GPS Error on Tracks

Independent testing and athlete reports consistently show GPS distance error of 1–3% per lap on a 400m track — 4–12 meters short per lap. Over a mile repeat (4 laps), that is 16–48 meters — enough to make a 6:00 mile look like 5:52 or 6:08 on the watch.

When GPS Is Acceptable on the Track

  • Easy warm-up and cool-down laps: Pace precision does not matter; GPS average is fine
  • Track Run mode (Garmin, COROS): Some watches have a dedicated track mode that learns the oval and improves accuracy after 2–3 laps — usable for reps after calibration
  • Heart rate data during reps: GPS pace may lie; HR data from a chest strap during track reps is still valuable for zone verification

Best Practices for Accurate Track Splits

  1. Use the track clock. A visible lap counter or stopwatch on the infield is ground truth.
  2. Manual lap at the same mark. Press lap at the 400m start line every rep — consistency matters more than exact line placement.
  3. Enable Track Mode if available. Garmin Track Run and COROS Track Mode improve after calibration laps.
  4. Log written splits. Record each rep time in a notebook or phone — do not rely on post-upload GPS data for track sessions.
  5. Compare GPS to track quarterly. Run 4 laps with manual lap at the line and compare to GPS total — note your personal offset for future reference.

For road intervals where GPS is reliable, see our Interval Training for Runners guide. The track is for measured work; the roads are for GPS-paced work — use each surface for its strength.

Programming Track Work Into Your Training Week

One track session every 7–14 days during intensity phases is sufficient for most recreational runners. More frequent track work increases injury risk without proportional fitness gain unless you are a competitive track athlete.

  • Base phase: No track work — focus on easy volume and strides on roads
  • Build phase (8–12 weeks out): One track session weekly — alternate 400m and 800m workouts
  • Peak phase (4–6 weeks out): One track session weekly — mile repeats and race-pace work
  • Taper (1–2 weeks out): One short track session (4 × 400m at race pace) for sharpness, then rest

Schedule track days at least 48 hours from long runs and 24 hours from other quality sessions. Tuesday or Wednesday track work with a Saturday long run is the most common placement. See the 5K Training Plan for a complete weekly structure.

FAQ

How often should I do track workouts?

One track session per week during build and peak phases — roughly 8–12 sessions total in a 5K training cycle. Two track sessions weekly is appropriate only for competitive athletes with high training volume (50+ mpw) and a multi-year base. More than one track session per week for recreational runners (under 40 mpw) increases injury risk without proportional benefit. Alternate workout types weekly: 400m reps one week, 800m the next, mile repeats the third.

Can I do track workouts if I am training for a half marathon or marathon?

Yes — mile repeats at half marathon pace and 800m repeats at 10K pace are valuable for half marathon preparation. Marathon training rarely requires track work beyond occasional mile repeats at marathon pace (which feel easy) or 400m strides. Half marathon and marathon athletes benefit from 4–6 track sessions during the 8-week build before their race, not throughout the entire training cycle.

Why are my GPS splits faster than my track clock splits?

GPS shortens curves on the oval — the watch thinks you ran less distance than you actually covered, so pace appears faster. Typical error is 1–3% short per lap. On a 6:00 mile, GPS may report 5:52–5:57. Enable Track Mode on your watch, use manual lap at the start line, or ignore GPS pace during reps and log track clock times instead.

What should I do if faster runners are using all the inner lanes?

Move to lanes 4–5 for your reps — the extra 15–20 meters per lap does not matter for training adaptation. Adjust your expectations: lane 4 adds ~3–4 seconds per 400m rep compared to lane 1. Log which lane you used so you can compare sessions consistently. Ask the coach or faster group if they will rotate lanes — most track communities share space when asked politely.
