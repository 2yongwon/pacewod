Half Marathon Pacing Strategy: First 10K Discipline and the Final 5K Push

Updated August 2026 · 12 min read

Mile 3 of a half marathon feels effortless. Crowds cheer, adrenaline spikes, and your watch shows you're 15 seconds ahead of goal pace. Mile 10 feels like a different sport. Legs heavy, breathing labored, and the final 5K becomes a survival shuffle instead of a controlled push. The difference isn't fitness — it's the first 10K.

The half marathon rewards restraint early and aggression late. Run the opening 6.2 miles too fast and glycogen depletion, lactate accumulation, and cardiac drift turn the final 5K into a time bank withdrawal with interest. Run them correctly and the back half becomes where PRs are made. This guide breaks half marathon pacing into three phases — first 5K, 5K to 10K, final 5K plus 1.1 miles — with checkpoint splits, HR guidance, and decision rules for race day.

Why the Half Marathon Is a Pacing Problem

A 5K rewards aggressive starts — the pain is short. A marathon demands conservative patience — the distance is long. The half marathon sits in the uncomfortable middle: long enough that early mistakes compound, short enough that athletes still start too fast because "it's only 13 miles."

Physiologically, half marathon pace sits near or slightly below lactate threshold for most runners — the intensity where lactate production equals clearance. Go above threshold in miles 1–4 and lactate accumulates faster than you clear it for the remaining hour. The result is progressive slowdown: negative split attempts become positive split reality.

Data from large race results consistently shows the fastest finishers negative split or even-split. Mid-pack and back-of-pack runners positive split by 3–8 minutes on average. The gap is pacing discipline, not training volume.

Expert Note

Your half marathon goal pace should feel slightly too easy at mile 2. If you think "I could run faster forever" at the 2-mile mark, you're probably on pace. If you think "this is perfect, I hope I can hold this," you're 10–15 seconds per mile fast and will pay at mile 10. The first 10K is a deposit; the final 5K is the withdrawal. Don't overdraw early.

Set Your Goal Pace From Evidence, Not Hope

Anchor goal pace to recent race results or validated time trials — not what you wish you could run. Use our Race Predictor with a recent 10K or 15K result for half marathon estimates. If your best recent 10K is 46:00 (7:24/mile), a 1:40 half (7:38/mile) is aggressive but reasonable; 1:35 (7:15/mile) requires perfect conditions and pacing.

Pace Sources Ranked by Reliability

  1. Recent half marathon within 6 months — adjust ±2–3 min for fitness change
  2. Recent 10K or 15K via race equivalence formulas
  3. Threshold tempo average — sustainable half marathon pace ≈ threshold pace + 10–20 sec/mile for most runners
  4. Long run pace — poor predictor alone; use only with race results cross-check

Phase 1: The First 5K (Miles 0–3.1)

Objective: settle into rhythm without exceeding goal pace. The first 5K is not free speed — it's the foundation.

Target Pace

Goal pace to goal pace + 5 sec/mile. For a 1:45 half (8:01/mile), run 8:01–8:06/mile. Do not chase faster splits because the corral is crowded or a downhill mile 1 flatters pace.

Common Mistakes

  • Adrenaline surge: first mile 20–30 sec fast — feels easy, costs 2–3 min later
  • Corral creep: faster runners pull you along; start in the correct corral or slightly conservative
  • Downhill delusion: bank time on early downhills; you'll repay on uphills or late miles
  • Watch obsession: checking pace every 30 seconds leads to micro-surges; check at mile markers only

Checkpoint: 5K Split

At 5K, cumulative time should be at or up to 15 seconds slower than even-split pace — never faster. Example for 1:45 goal (8:01/mile): 5K target 24:50–25:05. If you're sub 24:40, consciously slow miles 4–6.

Heart Rate Guidance

First 5K HR may read low (cold start, wrist sensor lag) or high (adrenaline). By mile 2, HR should stabilize below 85–88% max HR for goal pace — roughly high Zone 3 to low Zone 4 depending on your zones from the Heart Rate Zones Calculator. If HR is already at threshold in mile 2, you're running too fast regardless of pace display.

Phase 2: Miles 3.1 to 6.2 (5K to 10K)

Objective: lock into goal pace with metronomic consistency. This is the discipline phase — the segment that separates PRs from blow-ups.

Target Pace

Even goal pace ± 3 sec/mile. Minimal variation. Use course knowledge: planned 2–3 sec/mile slowdown on uphills, matching recovery on downhills — net even effort, not even pace.

The 10K Checkpoint: Go / No-Go Decision

10K is the most important split in the half marathon. At 10K, assess:

  • Split vs goal: within 15 sec of even pace = on track. 15–30 sec fast = risky, dial back. 30+ sec fast = blow-up likely, ease 5–10 sec/mile immediately.
  • RPE: should be 6–7/10 at 10K for goal pace — conversational is too easy; gasping is too hard.
  • HR: should be at or slightly below lactate threshold HR — stable, not climbing 5 bpm per mile.
  • Leg feel: light heaviness is normal; quad burn or calf cramp onset is a warning.

10K Split Examples

  • 1:30 goal10K target 42:30–42:45 · pace 6:52/mile
  • 1:40 goal10K target 47:15–47:30 · pace 7:37/mile
  • 1:50 goal10K target 51:45–52:00 · pace 8:20/mile
  • 2:00 goal10K target 56:30–56:45 · pace 9:06/mile

Nutrition at 10K

If the race exceeds 75 minutes for your pace, take gel or chews between 45–55 minutes (roughly mile 5–7), not at 10K when you're already struggling. Practice in long runs. One gel is sufficient for most half marathoners; two if finishing over 2:00 in heat.

Phase 3: The Final 5K Plus 1.1 Miles (10K to 13.1)

Objective: execute the push you've earned — or manage the damage if the first 10K was too fast.

Scenario A: On Pace at 10K

Miles 7–9: maintain goal pace. Do not surge yet — cardiac drift adds 3–5 bpm even at constant pace. Miles 10–12: assess. If RPE is 7–8 and HR stable, begin progressive push — 3–5 sec/mile faster each mile. Mile 13: empty the tank. Final 0.1 is free.

Negative split finish pattern: last 5K 15–45 seconds faster than first 5K cumulative — not mile 13 alone.

Scenario B: 15–30 Seconds Ahead at 10K

Bank the time by easing to goal pace + 5 sec/mile miles 7–9. Reassess at mile 10. If still feeling strong, gradual push miles 11–13. The banked seconds absorb cardiac drift; don't add more speed early in this phase.

Scenario C: Behind Pace at 10K

Determine why before surging. If HR is pegged and RPE is 9/10 — you started too fast; goal pace may be unrecoverable. Hold steady, run for strong finish, not suicidal negative split. If HR and RPE are moderate but pace was cautious — progressive push miles 7–13, target 5–8 sec/mile faster. Realistic recovery: 5–10 sec/mile faster over final 5K recovers ~30–60 seconds, not 3 minutes.

Scenario D: Way Ahead at 10K (30+ sec)

Slow immediately 10–15 sec/mile miles 7–8. Accept that PR may still happen but blow-up risk is high. Survival pacing — finish strong relative to how you feel, not relative to watch.

The Final 5K Mental Model

Treat miles 10–12 as a 5K race within the race. Pick a runner ahead, commit for 3 minutes, reassess. Repeat. Split the remaining distance into manageable chunks — mile 10, mile 11, mile 12, then "only a 5K left" at mile 8.1 (wait — at mile 8.1 you have 5K left; use that cue early).

Heart Rate vs Pace on Race Day

Pace is the primary guide for half marathon execution — the distance is short enough that cardiac drift is manageable but long enough that HR lags early and spikes late. Use HR as a secondary check:

  • Miles 1–3: ignore low wrist HR; trust pace and RPE
  • Miles 4–8: HR should plateau near threshold; climbing HR at constant pace = early overpace or heat stress
  • Miles 9–13: expect HR drift 3–8 bpm even at even pace — normal; don't slow to cap HR unless exceeding 92–94% max for extended periods

Chest strap recommended for race pacing if you've trained with one — wrist HR lags on surges and misreads in crowds with arm contact.

Course-Specific Adjustments

Hilly Courses

Pace by effort, not number. Allow 10–20 sec/mile slower on sustained climbs; recover 5–10 sec on descents — net even effort. At 10K checkpoint, compare cumulative time to goal, not mile-by-mile pace consistency.

Hot Weather

Add 1–3% time to goal (1:45 → 1:47–1:49). Start conservative — core temperature rises through the race. First 10K discipline matters more in heat; glycogen depletion accelerates with thermoregulatory stress.

Wind

Out-and-back: never bank tailwind miles. Run into headwind at goal pace + 5–10 sec; use tailwind for goal pace or slight negative split, not hero miles.

Training Pacing Rehearsals

Practice the strategy before race day:

  • Tempo runs: 4–6 miles at goal half marathon pace — confirms pace is sustainable
  • Progression long runs: first 8 miles easy, final 3–4 at goal pace — simulates fresh legs then race effort
  • 10K tune-up race: 4–6 weeks out — validates fitness and calibrates predictor
  • Negative split long run: second half of 12–14 mile run faster than first — builds late-race push habit

See Half Marathon Training Plan and Race Day Pacing Strategy for broader context.

Race Week and Morning Execution

  • Write splits on hand or watch: 5K, 10K, 15K, 20K cumulative targets
  • Start corral: line up at goal pace group, not aspirational group
  • First mile: hold back physically — count steps, run behind a pacer at goal time
  • 10K question: "Can I run 5 more miles at this feel?" If no, ease. If yes, prepare final push.
  • Post-10K: switch from discipline mode to execution mode — the work you saved is now spendable

Final Takeaway

Half marathon PRs are built in miles 4–8, not miles 12–13. The first 10K requires emotional restraint — running slower than you want when you feel great. The final 5K requires physical courage — spending the reserve you protected. Checkpoint splits at 5K and 10K are your go/no-go gates. Negative splits are not luck; they're the predictable result of disciplined early pacing.

FAQ

Should I negative split or even split my half marathon?

Even split is the safest default — same pace first and second half. Negative split (second half faster) is ideal when conditions are good and the first 10K was truly conservative. Positive split (first half faster) is the most common mistake and hardest to recover from. Aim for even to slightly negative; avoid banking time in the first 5K hoping to "make it up" later.

What should my 10K split be for a 1:45 half marathon?

Target 47:15–47:30 at 10K for a 1:45:00 finish (8:01/mile average). Even-split 10K is 47:23. If you're faster than 47:00 at 10K, ease pace unless RPE is still 6/10 or lower. Use our Pace Calculator for custom goal splits.

When should I start pushing the pace in a half marathon?

Begin a progressive push between miles 10 and 11 if 10K splits and RPE confirm you're on track — not before mile 8 unless you're significantly behind due to overly conservative early pacing. Miles 7–9 are for holding goal pace, not surging. The final 5K (mile 8.1 to 13.1) is the push window; mile 13 specifically should be your fastest mile if pacing was correct.

Is it okay to use a pace group for my first half marathon?

Yes, if the pacer targets your evidence-based goal time — not a aspirational stretch goal. Pace groups help first-timers hold back early. Verify the group's target matches your 10K predictor result. Start slightly behind the pacer for miles 1–3 to avoid corral surge, then settle in through mile 10. Do not follow a faster group because the crowd feels slow — first half discipline matters more than draft advantage.
