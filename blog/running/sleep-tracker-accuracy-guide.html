Sleep Tracker Accuracy: Garmin, Apple Watch, COROS, WHOOP, and Oura Compared

Updated August 12, 2026 · 14 min read

Your watch says 1 hour 12 minutes of deep sleep. Your ring says 48 minutes. Your recovery score is yellow because REM was “low.” You feel rested. The tempo is on the calendar. Sleep tracker accuracy has become a training decision problem as much as a product review problem — athletes are letting stage pie charts veto sessions that polysomnography (PSG), the clinical gold standard, would never claim to justify from a wrist optical sensor.

This guide compares what Garmin, Apple Watch, COROS, WHOOP, and Oura can and cannot measure, how total sleep time stacks up against stage detection, where readiness scores systematically mislead runners, and how to use wearable sleep data without outsourcing coaching to a black-box algorithm. Pair it with Sleep and Running Performance for physiology and habits, HRV for Runners for readiness trend rules, and Resting Heart Rate and Recovery for RHR baselines.

What “Accuracy” Means Against PSG

Polysomnography records brain waves (EEG), eye movements (EOG), muscle tone (EMG), respiration, and often oxygen saturation in a lab. Sleep stages — wake, N1, N2, N3 (deep), REM — are scored from neural signals. Consumer wearables have none of that. They infer sleep from movement (accelerometer), heart rate, heart rate variability (HRV), sometimes skin temperature and SpO2, then map those proxies onto stage labels that look like a sleep study report.

Two accuracy questions matter for athletes:

  • Total sleep time (TST) and sleep/wake detection: Did the device correctly identify when you were asleep versus awake or quietly reading in bed?
  • Stage classification: Within sleep, did it correctly label light, deep, and REM segments?

Published validation work consistently shows consumer devices are far better at the first question than the second. Typical TST error versus PSG often falls in a roughly 15–45 minute range depending on device, night, and population — useful for week-level trends. Stage agreement is much weaker: deep and REM boundaries are routinely misclassified, with error rates that make nightly “optimize for more REM” advice scientifically thin.

For training decisions, that hierarchy is the whole story. Duration and consistency are actionable. A single night’s deep-sleep percentage is mostly noise dressed as insight.

Stage Detection Limits vs PSG

Stage detection fails for structural reasons, not because one brand is uniquely careless. Quiet wakefulness looks like light sleep. Brief arousals may be missed. Deep sleep is associated with lower heart rate and less movement, but so is lying still while stressed. REM involves autonomic volatility that algorithms try to catch via HRV patterns — patterns that alcohol, illness, heat, and training load also scramble.

What Research Generally Finds

  • Wake vs sleep: Best agreement. Still imperfect for people who lie still while awake or move a lot while asleep.
  • Light sleep: Often over-labeled as a catch-all when the model is uncertain.
  • Deep (N3): Moderate correlation at best; nights can be under- or over-estimated by large margins.
  • REM: Frequently the weakest category on wrist devices; ring form factors sometimes reduce motion artifact but do not gain EEG.

None of this means stage charts are useless. Month-level shifts — less estimated deep sleep during a heat wave and high-mileage block, rebound after a deload — can corroborate what you already feel. It means you should not rearrange a workout because REM was 12 minutes “below target” on one chart.

Expert Note

Treat stage pie charts like estimated pace from GPS on a tree-covered trail: directionally informative over many samples, dangerous as a single-point truth. If your warm-up jog feels smooth, resting HR is near baseline, and total sleep was 7.5–8.5 hours, do not cancel a planned quality session because deep sleep was “only” 14%. Cross-check with RHR in the RHR guide and multi-day HRV trends in the HRV guide before you let a stage score edit the plan.

Total Sleep Time: The Reliable Signal

Total sleep time is the wearable sleep metric with the strongest practical reliability for runners. Devices that detect sleep onset and final wake within a reasonable window give you a duration trend you can act on: chronic nights under ~6.5–7 hours predict higher RPE, worse glucose handling, and elevated injury risk far more consistently than stage balance scores.

Where TST Still Goes Wrong

  • Time in bed vs time asleep: Some dashboards blur the two. Lying awake for 40 minutes after a late caffeine dose can look like a short night or a fragmented night depending on scoring rules.
  • Charging gaps: Apple Watch users who charge overnight create systematic undercounting unless Ultra or a careful schedule covers the sleep window.
  • Naps: Sync behavior varies; a 90-minute afternoon nap may or may not credit “recovery” depending on brand.
  • Partners, pets, travel: Motion from a shared bed can inflate wake time; airplanes and hotels confuse sleep windows.

Even with those caveats, a 14-day average of estimated TST is one of the highest-signal recovery inputs available without a lab. Prefer it over nightly stage perfectionism. Broader performance mechanisms — RPE inflation, glycogen handling, injury risk — are covered in Sleep and Running Performance.

Device-by-Device: What Each Platform Emphasizes

Garmin

Garmin estimates duration, stages (light, deep, REM, awake), respiration, and a Sleep Score that blends duration, stress during sleep, and stage balance. Overnight HRV feeds HRV Status; Body Battery folds sleep into a daily energy proxy. For runners already in the Garmin ecosystem, the value is integration with Training Status and load — not PSG-grade stages.

Strengths: continuous overnight wear for most athletes; strong duration trends; HRV Status as a multi-day readiness lens.

Weaknesses: Sleep Score can punish normal brief awakenings; stage error same class as peers; alcohol and poor fit distort HRV-linked scores.

Apple Watch

Apple tracks time in bed, asleep, and stages (REM, Core, Deep) on supported models, with sleep schedule features that often improve behavior more than stage precision. Third-party apps layer readiness scoring on top of Apple’s raw sleep and HR data.

Strengths: frictionless logging for iPhone users; schedule consistency nudges; decent TST when worn all night.

Weaknesses: charging conflicts on non-Ultra hardware; stage accuracy not clinical; readiness depends heavily on which third-party model you trust.

COROS

COROS provides sleep duration and quality estimates tied into EvoLab recovery context. Runners choosing COROS for GPS and training load usually treat sleep as a supporting metric, not the product’s center of gravity.

Strengths: overnight wear habits similar to Garmin; useful duration + recovery framing for training blocks.

Weaknesses: stage labels inherit the same accelerometer/HR inference limits; less sleep-product marketing than WHOOP or Oura does not mean higher stage validity.

WHOOP

WHOOP is recovery-first: strain versus sleep need, heavy use of overnight HRV, and coaching language that can feel absolute. Athletes who buy WHOOP often buy the narrative — green means go, red means stop — which is exactly when score misuse spikes.

Strengths: consistent overnight HR sampling; clear recovery/strain framing; useful for athletes who previously ignored sleep duration.

Weaknesses: proprietary sleep need and recovery math can over-penalize life stress and alcohol; stage/REM storytelling exceeds what wrist PPG can prove; subscription framing encourages daily overreaction.

Oura

Oura’s ring reduces some wrist motion artifact and emphasizes readiness, sleep score, and temperature trends. Temperature deviation can flag illness earlier than a stage chart. Form factor helps compliance for people who hate sleeping in a watch.

Strengths: wear comfort; temperature as a complementary signal; strong schedule and duration visibility.

Weaknesses: still no EEG — stages are estimated; readiness can conflict with subjective feel and planned periodization; finger perfusion and fit still matter.

When Sleep Scores Mislead Training Decisions

Scores mislead when athletes confuse a composite index with a measurement. A Sleep Score or Recovery percentage mixes duration, estimated stages, HRV, resting HR, and sometimes respiratory rate into one number. Opaque weighting means you cannot tell whether last night’s yellow came from 40 minutes less sleep, a restless partner, a glass of wine, or a warm bedroom.

High-Risk Misuse Patterns

  • Vetoing quality after one yellow night: One short night after a late dinner rarely ruins a well-tapered session. Three short nights plus rising RHR does.
  • Adding volume on green: High readiness is permission to execute the plan, not a demand to double the workout.
  • Chasing REM percentage: No consumer device gives you reliable enough REM to justify supplements, gadgets, or anxiety rituals aimed at “fixing” a chart.
  • Ignoring legs when the score is green: Local muscle damage, DOMS, and neural fatigue from hills or track work do not always crash overnight HRV the next morning.
  • Trusting sleep score after alcohol: Subjective “I slept hard” plus suppressed REM and elevated HR produces contradictory dashboards — the workout cost is real either way.

A Decision Rule That Survives Bad Algorithms

  1. Check total sleep time vs your 14-day average.
  2. Check resting HR vs baseline (same conditions).
  3. Check HRV trend over 3–7 days, not one morning — see HRV for Runners.
  4. Check warm-up honesty: stride smoothness, first-mile RPE, motivation.
  5. Only then let a composite sleep/readiness score modulate intensity at the margins (5–10% trim), not cancel the week.

Comparing Brands Without Marketing Noise

Head-to-head “which is most accurate?” debates often miss the athlete’s real choice. If you already train with Garmin or COROS, adding Oura may improve sleep compliance and temperature trends, but it will not give you PSG. If you want recovery coaching language, WHOOP and Oura lean harder into it — which helps behavior change and increases false-precision risk. Apple wins on ecosystem convenience when charged correctly overnight.

Practical ranking for runners who care about training decisions:

  • Best primary use of any of them: 7–14 day TST and schedule consistency.
  • Best secondary use: overnight HRV/RHR trends tied to load (Garmin/COROS/WHOOP/Oura all play here with different UX).
  • Weakest use across all of them: nightly stage micro-management and single-morning hard vetoes.

For how sleep restriction actually changes endurance performance — independent of brand — stay with Sleep and Running Performance. For interpreting the autonomic numbers that sleep scores partially reflect, use HRV for Runners and Resting Heart Rate and Recovery.

How to Audit Your Own Tracker in Two Weeks

You do not need a sleep lab to sanity-check a device. Run a simple field audit:

  • Log bedtime, estimated sleep onset, and wake time manually for 14 nights.
  • Note alcohol, late caffeine, illness, travel, and hard sessions.
  • Compare device TST to your manual window; flag nights with >45 minute disagreements.
  • Ignore stage percentages for decisions during the audit; watch whether duration trends match how easy runs feel.
  • On mornings with conflicting green score + heavy legs, write down which one predicted the workout better by noon.

Most athletes discover the same pattern: duration tracks performance better than stages; multi-day autonomic trends beat single scores; subjective warm-up still wins ties.

Final Takeaway

Garmin, Apple Watch, COROS, WHOOP, and Oura all estimate sleep. None observe brain sleep stages. Total sleep time and schedule consistency are reliable enough to guide recovery habits and to flag chronic debt. Stage detection versus PSG is limited enough that nightly REM/deep targets should not drive training edits. Composite sleep and recovery scores are useful trend dashboards and poor day-of coaches.

Buy or keep the device you will wear all night. Optimize the behaviors that move duration and consistency. Let HRV and RHR trends corroborate fatigue. Protect planned quality unless multiple signals — short sleep streak, elevated RHR, falling HRV, ugly warm-up — agree. The accurate part of sleep tracking is the trend. The inaccurate part is the story your app tells about one night’s stages.

FAQ

Which sleep tracker is most accurate: Garmin, Apple, COROS, WHOOP, or Oura?

For total sleep time, modern versions of all five are generally in a usable band versus PSG when worn correctly all night — often within tens of minutes on average, with larger errors on individual nights. No consumer option matches lab stage scoring. Choose based on wear compliance and ecosystem (training watch vs ring vs strap), then judge the device by duration trends and overnight HRV/RHR consistency, not by which brand draws the prettiest REM chart.

Are wearable sleep stages accurate enough to change workouts?

No — not from a single night. Stage classification error versus polysomnography is large enough that deep/REM percentages are weak daily inputs. Use stages, if at all, for multi-week pattern spotting during heavy blocks or illness. Change workouts when total sleep, resting HR, HRV trend, and warm-up feel align — the framework in Sleep and Running Performance.

Why does my sleep score look fine when I feel exhausted?

Composite scores overweight signals they can measure (HRV, estimated stages, duration) and underweight signals they cannot (local muscle damage, monotony, life stress, under-fueling). Green after an eccentric-heavy downhill long run is common. Trust legs and easy-run RPE when they conflict with a high score, and review RHR baselines in the RHR guide.

Should runners buy a dedicated sleep ring if they already have a watch?

Only if overnight watch wear is inconsistent or you want temperature trends and simpler sleep-focused UX. A second device does not unlock clinical stage accuracy. One well-worn tracker plus honest duration targets beats two dashboards you obsess over. If your watch already captures overnight HRV and TST, invest the money in earlier bedtimes and a cooler room first.
