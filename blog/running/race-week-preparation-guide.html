Race Week Preparation: Taper, Nutrition, Sleep, and What to Skip

Updated August 2026 · 14 min read

Seven days out and your legs feel like concrete. You haven't run hard in ten days. You are tempted to "test" fitness with a few threshold reps. You are also tempted to eat an entire pasta pot, buy new shoes, and scroll race results until 1 a.m. Every one of those impulses is a race-week mistake that a structured countdown prevents.

Race week is not training week. Fitness is already built — the goal is to arrive at the start line rested, fueled, mentally calm, and equipped. The runners who PR execute a boring week: reduced volume, maintained sharpness, practiced nutrition, early sleep, and disciplined avoidance of panic decisions.

This guide walks through a 7-day countdown — taper volume cut of 40–60%, carb loading nuance, gear check, sleep priority, and the anxiety-driven mistakes to skip.

The 7-Day Race Week Countdown

Adjust day labels to your race date. This template assumes a Sunday race; shift accordingly for Saturday or weekday events.

Day 7 (Monday): Enter taper fully

  • Volume at 50–60% of peak week; no hard efforts beyond short strides
  • One optional short quality touch — 2–3 × 2 min at race pace with full recovery (race-pace reminder, not a workout)
  • Begin normal carb-forward meals; no extreme loading yet
  • Gear audit: lay out race kit, verify shoe mileage, check watch charge

Day 6 (Tuesday): Maintain routine

  • 30–45 min easy run or rest day if legs feel heavy
  • 4–6 × 20 sec strides post-run if fresh
  • Hydration baseline: pale yellow urine target
  • Confirm travel, parking, corral, and start time logistics

Day 5 (Wednesday): Last moderate day

  • 20–30 min easy with 3–4 × 1 min at race pace — final neuromuscular tune-up
  • Volume ~40–50% of peak
  • Review pacing plan — splits, HR ceiling, aid station strategy
  • Avoid new foods, new shoes, new gels

Day 4 (Thursday): Carb emphasis begins

  • 20–30 min easy jog or cross-train; strides optional
  • Increase carbohydrate portion at meals — rice, pasta, potatoes, bread — without force-feeding
  • Reduce fiber and fat slightly to prevent GI distress
  • Pin race bib if picked up; organize morning layout

Day 3 (Friday): Protect legs

  • 15–20 min shakeout jog or full rest
  • 4 × 15 sec strides if jogging; skip if resting
  • Continue carb emphasis; normal protein; moderate sodium
  • Early bedtime target begins tonight

Day 2 (Saturday): Pre-race prep

  • 10–15 min jog max — or walk — plus 2–3 strides
  • Familiar breakfast timing rehearsal (same as race morning plan)
  • Lay out kit: shoes, socks, singlet, bib, watch, gels, safety pins, throwaway layer
  • Charge watch; set multiple alarms; plan 90+ min arrival buffer
  • Light carb dinner, low fiber, early sleep — 8–9 hours target

Day 1 (Race Day)

  • Wake 3–4 hours before start; familiar breakfast 2–3 hours pre-gun
  • 150–250 ml fluid with optional electrolytes 60–90 min before
  • 10–15 min warm-up jog + drills + 2–4 strides (distance-dependent)
  • Execute pacing plan — race week prep pays off here
Taper volume rule

Cut total weekly volume 40–60% from peak training week while maintaining short race-pace touches. Fitness does not improve in race week — only freshness does. Trust the taper even when legs feel heavy (normal glycogen and water retention). See Tapering Before a Race for the full physiology.

Taper Execution: Volume Cut Without Detraining

Taper is the bridge between training block and race performance. Meta-analyses show structured tapers improve performance 2–3% on average — roughly 2–6 minutes on a marathon, 1–3 minutes on a half, 30–90 seconds on a 10K. The mechanism: reduced fatigue with maintained neuromuscular fitness and glycogen supercompensation.

Race week taper specifics:

  • Volume: 40–60% reduction from peak week mileage
  • Intensity: maintain — short race-pace segments prevent "stale legs" feeling
  • Frequency: keep run frequency similar; shorten duration
  • Strength: skip heavy lifting; mobility and light stretching only

Heavy legs during taper are normal — not a sign you need more running. Water and glycogen retention in recovering muscle tissue increases perceived weight. Race day adrenaline resolves the sensation within the first mile.

Distance-specific taper length (full taper may begin before race week):

  • 5K–10K: 7–10 day taper; race week is the final phase
  • Half marathon: 10–14 day taper; race week is days 7–0
  • Marathon: 14–21 day taper; race week is the sharp end of a longer reduction

For deep taper science, see Tapering Before a Race.

Carb Loading: Nuance Over Pasta Parties

Carb loading maximizes muscle glycogen stores before events over 75–90 minutes. For 5K and 10K, normal meals suffice — glycogen depletion is not the limiter. For half marathon and marathon, structured loading matters.

Modern protocol (avoid the old depletion phase):

  1. Days 6–4 before race: normal training diet, 5–7 g carbs/kg body weight
  2. Days 3–1 before race: increase to 8–10 g carbs/kg; reduce fiber and fat to prevent GI issues
  3. Race eve dinner: familiar foods, moderate portion — not a binge. Wake hungry, not stuffed.
  4. Race morning: 1–4 g carbs/kg 2–4 hours before start — toast, oatmeal, banana, sports drink

Common carb loading mistakes:

  • One-night pasta binge: causes bloating and poor sleep without full glycogen benefit — loading takes 48–72 hours
  • New foods: race week is not the time for exotic cuisine or high-fiber "health" meals
  • Excess fat and protein: displace carb calories and slow digestion
  • Under-eating from nerves: anxiety kills appetite; force familiar carb sources even if unexciting

5K racers: follow the 5K Race Strategy nutrition notes — light familiar breakfast, no loading required. Marathon racers: align with Marathon Pacing Strategy fueling timeline for race-morning gel and aid station plans practiced in training.

Gear Check: Nothing New on Race Day

Race week gear audit — complete by Day 5:

  • Shoes: racing flats or trainers with 50–200 miles on them — not box-fresh, not dead (>400 mi)
  • Socks: tested on long runs; no seam surprises
  • Kit: weather-appropriate layers tested in training; pin bib to outermost layer
  • Watch: charged, goal pace alert or screen configured, HR strap battery checked
  • Nutrition: exact gels/chews planned for race; flavor and quantity practiced in last long run
  • Anti-chafe: body glide on known hot spots
  • Throwaway layer: old long sleeve for cold starts if corral wait is long
  • Post-race: dry clothes, recovery snack, sandals in drop bag

The "nothing new on race day" rule includes shoes, socks, nutrition, watch settings, and breakfast. If you haven't tested it in the last three weeks, it doesn't belong in the race.

Sleep: The Free Performance Enhancer

Sleep deprivation impairs pacing judgment, increases RPE, and reduces glycogen utilization efficiency. One poor night hurts; race week chronic short sleep accumulates.

Sleep targets:

  • Days 7–3: normal 7–9 hours — bank sleep before pre-race anxiety hits
  • Days 2–1: 8–9 hours target; accept that pre-race night may be 6–7 hours (normal)
  • Race eve: if sleep is poor, performance impact is modest if prior nights were solid — do not panic

Sleep hygiene: reduce alcohol ( disrupts REM), limit screen time after 9 p.m., cool dark room, consistent wake time. Avoid late heavy meals and excessive hydration after 8 p.m. on race eve — bathroom breaks destroy sleep.

See Sleep and Running Performance for the full evidence on sleep debt and endurance outcomes.

Anxiety Mistakes: What to Skip

Pre-race anxiety drives counterproductive decisions. Skip these:

  • Fitness test workouts: "I need to know I'm ready" threshold sessions 3–5 days out — they add fatigue, not confidence
  • Volume panic: adding miles because taper feels wrong — trust the 40–60% cut
  • Exhibition stretching or foam rolling marathons: aggressive new mobility irritates tissue before race
  • New gear purchases: shoes, watch, belt, hydration pack — all tested in training or not at all
  • Excessive carb loading day before: bloating and GI distress from one massive dinner
  • Hyperhydration: chugging water without sodium — hyponatremia risk, bloated stomach
  • Course recon by running hard: easy jog or drive the course; don't turn recon into a workout
  • Pace obsession on easy jogs: shakeout runs should feel effortless; checking pace every 30 sec adds stress
  • Negative visualization spiral: scrolling DNF stories and weather doom — prepare contingencies once, then stop

Anxiety is energy. Redirect it into logistics: packing, timing, pacing card, breakfast plan. Control the controllable; the start gun handles the rest.

Distance-Specific Race Week Notes

5K

Minimal taper — 3–5 days volume reduction may suffice. Race-pace strides Wednesday and Friday. No carb load. Review 5K Race Strategy for pacing and warm-up.

10K

7-day taper standard. One race-pace session mid-week. Familiar breakfast; gel optional. Pacing plan with km splits ready.

Half Marathon

10–14 day taper; race week is final 40–60% volume cut. Carb emphasis days 3–1. Long warm-up (15–20 min) race morning. See Half Marathon Pacing Strategy for split strategy.

Marathon

14–21 day taper; race week feels very easy. Aggressive carb load days 3–1. Nothing left to gain — protect legs. Full pacing and fueling plan from Marathon Pacing Strategy.

Race Morning Execution Checklist

  1. Wake 3–4 hours pre-start; eat tested breakfast
  2. 150–250 ml fluid 60–90 min before; small sip 15 min before if dry mouth
  3. Arrive 60–90 min early — bathroom, bag drop, corral
  4. 10–20 min warm-up (distance-dependent) + drills + strides
  5. Lock pacing plan in head: km 1 conservative, middle disciplined, finish committed
  6. Start watch at gun or when crossing mat — know which

Final Takeaway

Race week is execution, not improvement. Cut volume 40–60%, keep short race-pace touches, load carbs gradually over 48–72 hours for long events, audit gear by mid-week, bank sleep early, and resist anxiety-driven mistakes. The training is done. A boring, disciplined race week is the highest-probability path to the result you trained for.

FAQ

How much should I reduce mileage during race week?

Cut total weekly volume 40–60% from your peak training week. Maintain run frequency but shorten each session. Include 2–3 short race-pace segments across the week to stay sharp. Do not add extra miles because legs feel heavy — that sensation is normal taper physiology, not lost fitness.

When should I start carb loading?

For half marathon and marathon, begin increasing carbohydrates 3 days before the race — target 8–10 g/kg body weight daily, with reduced fiber and fat. For 5K and 10K, normal carb-forward meals are sufficient; no special loading protocol needed. Avoid one-night pasta binges; effective loading takes 48–72 hours.

Why do my legs feel heavy during race week?

Taper heaviness is normal. Reduced training load triggers glycogen and water retention in muscles, creating a stiff, heavy sensation. Neuromuscular freshness lags behind perceived leg weight. Race day adrenaline and the first mile typically resolve the feeling. Do not interpret heavy legs as a signal to run more.

What should I avoid doing during race week?

Avoid fitness test workouts, new shoes or gear, new foods or gels, excessive volume, aggressive new stretching, one-night carb binges, hyperhydration with plain water, and hard course recon runs. Nothing new on race day — if you haven't tested it in training in the last three weeks, skip it.
